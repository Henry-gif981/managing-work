## [intro-1] 1 Rationale for the Research

## [intro-1.1] 1.1 The role of multilateral development finance for developing economies
<!-- page:4 -->

<!-- p:1 page:4 -->
For developing economies, the need for long-term investment in infrastructure, energy, health, and education far exceeds what domestic resources can provide. Savings are limited, and tax bases are narrow. Borrowing on international markets often comes at a high cost or with short maturities, if it is available at all. This is where multilateral development banks (MDBs) step in. Backed by member-state capital and strong credit ratings, they can raise funds cheaply in global markets and pass those advantages on to borrowing countries through longer maturities and lower interest rates.

<!-- p:2 page:4 -->
MDBs' most direct contribution is stable, long-term capital, which suits infrastructure projects that may take decades to pay back. This stability matters most in times of crisis. During the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic, private capital withdrew, while MDBs expanded their lending to keep essential investment going. Beyond their own funds, MDBs also play a catalytic role. By co-financing, offering guarantees and sharing risk, they make projects more attractive to commercial banks and private investors who might otherwise hold back.

<!-- p:3 page:4 -->
Their value also goes beyond finance. Through project appraisal, technical assistance, and international standards on procurement and safeguards, MDBs help raise project quality and build the capacity of borrowing governments. Their multilateral nature also allows them to coordinate action on shared challenges such as climate change and regional connectivity.

## [intro-1.2] 1.2 Why the IBRD matters for Vietnam after its shift from concessional IDA lending to IBRD lending
<!-- page:4 -->

<!-- p:1 page:4 -->
For many years, Vietnam relied on IDA, the World Bank's window for low-income countries, which offers loans with very low interest rates and long repayment periods. As the economy grew, this changed. The Bank declared Vietnam IBRD-eligible in 2007, and the first IBRD loan, a US$500 million Public Investment Reform loan, was approved in December 2009 [1; 2]. Vietnam then graduated from IDA in July 2017 and no longer receives regular IDA credits. From 2018, it could borrow only from the IBRD and through non-concessional IDA terms, both at higher interest rates, with transitional support during the IDA18 period (FY18–20) [3]. In July 2026, the World Bank reclassified Vietnam as an upper-middle-income economy [4].

<!-- p:2 page:5 -->
This shift makes the IBRD Vietnam's main source of World Bank financing. IBRD loans are more expensive than IDA credits, but they are still cheaper and longer-term than most commercial loans. That matters because Vietnam's investment needs keep growing. According to the World Bank, official financing alone will not be enough to meet these needs, so Vietnam will also have to borrow on capital markets and pay more to do so [3]. In this setting, the IBRD offers a middle path: funding on reasonable terms for large, long-term projects that the market may not support on its own.

<!-- p:3 page:5 -->
Higher borrowing costs also mean Vietnam must choose and manage projects more carefully. After graduation, the Government asked the Ministry of Planning and Investment to report on how to mobilize loans in the future, especially non-concessional loans. The IBRD can help here. Beyond money, it brings project appraisal, technical advice and international standards, which help Vietnam use each loan more effectively.

<!-- p:4 page:5 -->
For these reasons, understanding how the IBRD works, including its lending terms, procedures and priorities, is important for Vietnam as it moves into a new stage of development finance.

## [intro-3.1] 3.1. To analyze the mechanisms of IBRD involvement in project financing
<!-- page:6 -->

<!-- p:1 page:6 -->
The first objective is to examine how the IBRD takes part in financing projects, from raising funds to supporting projects on the ground. On the funding side, the study looks at how the IBRD mobilizes capital through its member countries’ contributions and through bond issues on international markets, and how this allows it to lend at favorable terms. On the lending side, it reviews the main instruments the IBRD uses, such as investment project financing, development policy financing, program-for-results, and guarantees, and explains when each is applied.

<!-- p:2 page:6 -->
The study also follows the IBRD project cycle, which includes identification, preparation, appraisal, negotiation and approval, implementation and supervision, and completion and evaluation. This helps show how the IBRD shares responsibilities with the borrowing government at each stage, and how it works with co-financiers and private investors to mobilize additional capital. Through this analysis, the study aims to clarify not only what the IBRD finances but also how its involvement shapes the quality and outcomes of projects.

<!-- page:6 -->

<!-- p:1 page:6 -->
The second objective is to see how the IBRD’s mechanisms work in practice in Vietnam. The study also looks at the difficulties that arose during implementation and how they were handled. Through this, the study aims to show what IBRD financing can bring to Vietnam in its new stage of borrowing after IDA, and to draw lessons for future IBRD projects.

## [ch1] CHAPTER 1: OVERVIEW OF THE IBRD
<!-- page:10 -->

## [1.1] 1.1. What is the IBRD?
<!-- page:10 -->

## [1.1.1] 1.1.1. Definition, mission, and eligible borrowers
<!-- page:10 -->

<!-- p:1 page:10 -->
The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD) is a global development cooperative owned by 189 member countries. It is one of the five institutions of the World Bank Group and, together with IDA, forms what is commonly called the World Bank [7]. Although it is called a bank, the IBRD does not aim to maximize profit like a commercial bank. Instead, it raises funds on international capital markets and lends them to member countries for development purposes. Its role goes beyond lending: it provides loans, guarantees, risk management products and advisory services, and it also coordinates responses to regional and global challenges.

<!-- p:2 page:10 -->
The IBRD shares the World Bank Group's mission, which is to end extreme poverty and boost shared prosperity on a livable planet. Job creation is now seen as central to this mission and as a key driver of sustainable development. In practice, this means the IBRD supports projects and reforms that help countries grow in a sustainable and inclusive way.

<!-- p:3 page:10 -->
The IBRD's main borrowers are middle-income countries. It also lends to creditworthy poorer countries that are also eligible for IDA support, often through a mix of IBRD and IDA funding. Borrowers are usually national governments, but the IBRD can also lend to public enterprises or local authorities, provided that the national government guarantees the loan. A country's eligibility depends mainly on its income level and its creditworthiness. As a country grows richer, it moves from IDA to IBRD lending, as Vietnam did after 2017.

## [1.1.2] 1.1.2. Position within the World Bank Group; distinguishing IBRD, IDA, IFC, MIGA, and ICSID
<!-- page:10 -->

<!-- p:1 page:10 -->
The World Bank Group consists of five institutions. Each has its own role, but they work together toward the same goal. The IBRD is the oldest member of the Group, founded in 1944, and it is also the largest development bank in the world. Together with IDA, it forms the World Bank in the narrow sense. The IBRD aims to reduce poverty in middle-income and creditworthy poorer countries, while IDA focuses on the world's poorest countries. The other three institutions support this work: IFC and MIGA focus on the private sector, while ICSID deals with investment disputes. The main differences among the five institutions are summarized in Table 1.

<!-- table:T1 page:11 -->
**Table 1. The five institutions of the World Bank Group**

| Institution | Year founded | Main clients | Main activities | Source of funds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| IBRD | 1944 | Governments of middle-income and creditworthy poorer countries | Loans, guarantees, risk management, advisory services | Bonds on international capital markets, member capital |
| IDA | 1960 | Governments of the poorest countries | Grants and very low-interest or interest-free loans | Contributions from donor countries |
| IFC | 1956 | Private enterprises in developing countries | Loans, equity investment, advisory services | Own capital and market borrowing |
| MIGA | 1988 | Foreign investors and lenders | Insurance against political risks such as war or expropriation | Member capital and premiums |
| ICSID | 1966 | Governments and foreign investors | Settling investment disputes through arbitration and conciliation | Contributions from the World Bank and fees |

*Source: Authors' compilation from [7] and [8].*

<!-- p:2 page:11 -->
The key difference between the IBRD and IDA lies in the type of borrower and the cost of funds. IBRD loans carry market-based interest rates, while IDA offers much cheaper terms because it is funded by donors. The IBRD and IFC differ mainly in their clients. The IBRD lends to governments or to entities guaranteed by governments, while IFC invests directly in private companies without a government guarantee. MIGA and ICSID do not provide finance directly. Instead, they reduce risks for investors and help settle disputes, which makes the investment climate more attractive.

<!-- p:3 page:12 -->
In this structure, the IBRD acts as the main channel for public financing to middle-income countries. This is why it is especially important for Vietnam today.

## [1.2] 1.2. History and development
<!-- page:12 -->

<!-- p:1 page:12 -->
The history of the IBRD can be divided into four main stages, each reflecting changes in the global economy and in the needs of member countries.

<!-- p:2 page:12 -->
Founding and post-war reconstruction (1944–1950s). The IBRD was created at the Bretton Woods Conference in July 1944, together with the International Monetary Fund, as part of the effort to rebuild the world economy after the Second World War. It began operations in 1946. Its first loan, worth US$250 million, went to France in 1947 to support reconstruction [9]. In the same year, the IBRD issued its first bonds on the US market, laying the foundation for its funding model. However, when the United States launched the Marshall Plan in 1948, much of Europe's reconstruction was financed through that program. This led the IBRD to turn its attention to developing countries [8].

<!-- p:3 page:12 -->
Shift to development and expansion (1950s–1970s). In this period, the IBRD mainly financed large infrastructure projects such as power plants, roads, railways and irrigation systems in Asia, Africa and Latin America. As its work grew, new institutions were created to meet different needs: IFC in 1956 to support the private sector, and IDA in 1960 to serve the poorest countries. This allowed the IBRD to focus on middle-income economies. From the late 1960s, under President Robert McNamara, the IBRD placed greater emphasis on poverty reduction, rural development, education and health, and its lending grew rapidly [8].

<!-- p:4 page:12 -->
Crisis and reform (1980s–1990s). The debt crisis of the 1980s hit many developing countries hard. In response, the IBRD introduced structural adjustment lending, which provided funds in exchange for economic reforms such as reducing budget deficits and opening markets. These programs helped some countries stabilize but were also criticized for their social costs. In the 1990s, the IBRD paid more attention to governance, anti-corruption, the environment and social safeguards.

<!-- p:5 page:13 -->
The 21st century (2000–present). The IBRD aligned its work with global development goals and played a counter-cyclical role during crises, sharply increasing lending during the 2008 global financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. In 2013, the World Bank Group adopted two goals: ending extreme poverty and boosting shared prosperity. More recently, through its reform process, the IBRD has expanded its mission to address global challenges such as climate change. Today, its mission is to end extreme poverty and boost shared prosperity on a livable planet, with job creation at its center [8].

<!-- p:6 page:13 -->
After more than 80 years, the IBRD has changed from a reconstruction bank for Europe into a global development institution. Its funding model of borrowing on capital markets and lending to member countries has stayed the same, while its focus has steadily widened to meet new development needs.

## [1.3] 1.3. Organizational structure
<!-- page:13 -->

## [1.3.1] 1.3.1. Board of Governors, Board of Executive Directors, President
<!-- page:13 -->

<!-- p:1 page:13 -->
Board of Governors. The Board of Governors is the highest decision-making body of the IBRD. Each member country appoints one Governor and one Alternate Governor, usually the minister of finance or the governor of the central bank. The Board meets once a year at the Annual Meetings of the World Bank Group and the IMF. It keeps the power to make the most important decisions, such as admitting new members, increasing or reducing capital, and approving the annual financial statements. Because it meets only once a year, it delegates most of its day-to-day powers to the Board of Executive Directors.

<!-- p:2 page:13 -->
Board of Executive Directors. The Board of Executive Directors is made up of the President and 25 Executive Directors. It works at the IBRD's headquarters in Washington, D.C., and meets several times a week. The Board approves loans, guarantees and the annual budget, sets operational policies and reviews country strategies. A few large shareholders appoint their own Executive Director, while most member countries are grouped into constituencies that elect one Executive Director to represent them. Decisions are usually made by consensus, but formal votes are weighted by each country's shareholding.

<!-- p:3 page:14 -->
President. The President chairs the Board of Executive Directors and leads the entire World Bank Group. The President is selected by the Executive Directors for a five-year term, which can be renewed. The President does not vote except to break a tie. The President is responsible for managing staff and daily operations, and also proposes loans and policies to the Board for approval. The current President is Ajay Banga, who began his term on 2 June 2023 [10].

<!-- p:4 page:14 -->
In short, the Board of Governors sets the overall direction, the Board of Executive Directors turns it into policies and approves operations, and the President carries them out. This division of roles helps balance the interests of member countries with the need for efficient management.

## [1.3.2] 1.3.2. Voting power based on shareholding and membership
<!-- page:14 -->

<!-- p:1 page:14 -->
To become a member of the IBRD, a country must first be a member of the International Monetary Fund. It must then subscribe to shares of the IBRD's capital. The number of shares a country receives depends mainly on the size of its economy. Only a small part of this capital is actually paid in. The larger part is callable capital, which the IBRD can ask members to pay only if it cannot meet its own debt obligations. This callable capital acts as a guarantee that supports the IBRD's strong credit rating.

<!-- p:2 page:14 -->
Voting power in the IBRD follows the principle of one share, one vote, rather than one country, one vote as in the United Nations. Each member's votes consist of two parts. The first part is basic votes, which are shared equally among all members to give smaller countries a minimum voice. The second part is share votes, with one vote for each share held. As a result, countries that contribute more capital have more influence over decisions.

<!-- p:3 page:14 -->
This system gives the largest shareholders considerable power. The United States holds the largest share of votes, at around 15–16 percent, followed by Japan, China, Germany, France and the United Kingdom. Because some major decisions, such as amending the Articles of Agreement, require an 85 percent majority, the United States effectively has a veto over them. To make the system fairer, the IBRD carried out voting reforms in 2008–2010 and later capital increases. These raised basic votes and shifted more voting power to developing and transition countries.

<!-- p:4 page:15 -->
Vietnam's voting power remains small. As of April 2026, Vietnam held 0.21 percent of IBRD votes [11]. In practice, Vietnam takes part in decisions through the constituency it belongs to, which shares one Executive Director on the Board. This means Vietnam's influence depends partly on cooperation with other members of its group.

## [1.3.3] 1.3.3. Source of funds: paid-in capital and bond issuance on capital markets
<!-- page:15 -->

<!-- p:1 page:15 -->
The IBRD funds its lending mainly from two sources: capital from its member countries and money raised by issuing bonds on international capital markets. In addition, loan repayments and the IBRD's own earnings provide further resources.

<!-- p:2 page:15 -->
Paid-in capital. When a country joins the IBRD, it subscribes to a certain number of shares. Only a small part of this subscribed capital is actually paid in cash. The rest is callable capital, which members promise to pay only if the IBRD cannot meet its obligations to bondholders. This call has never been made in the IBRD's history. Paid-in capital, together with retained earnings, forms the IBRD's equity. This equity acts as a cushion against losses and determines how much the IBRD can safely lend. When demand for lending grows, member countries can agree to a capital increase, as they did in 2018 [12].

<!-- p:3 page:15 -->
Bond issuance. The largest source of funds comes from borrowing on international capital markets. The IBRD acts as a financial intermediary: it raises funds from global markets and lends them to its member countries. It issues bonds in many currencies and sells them to investors such as central banks, pension funds and insurance companies around the world. Because of its strong capital base, its callable capital and its careful financial management, the IBRD has held the highest credit rating (AAA) for decades. This allows it to borrow at very low interest rates and pass these savings on to borrowers through loans with long maturities and reasonable costs. In recent years, the IBRD has also issued sustainable development bonds and green bonds, linking investors' money to social and environmental goals [13].

<!-- p:4 page:16 -->
Other sources. Loan repayments are recycled into new loans, and the IBRD's net income from lending and investments helps strengthen its capital. More recently, the IBRD has also tried new tools, such as hybrid capital and guarantees from shareholders, to increase its lending capacity without large capital increases.

<!-- p:5 page:16 -->
This funding model is the main difference between the IBRD and IDA. IDA depends on regular donations from rich countries, while the IBRD can fund itself through the market. As a result, the IBRD can lend on a large scale at low cost, but its loans are more expensive than IDA credits.

## [1.4] 1.4. Financing instruments and loan conditions
<!-- page:16 -->

<!-- p:1 page:16 -->
The IBRD lends through three instruments, each defined by what it finances and what triggers disbursement. A single loan product, the IBRD Flexible Loan, sets the price and repayment terms. Environmental, social and procurement rules then govern how the money may be spent.

## [1.4.1] 1.4.1. Three main instruments: Investment Project Financing (IPF), Development Policy Financing (DPF) and Program-for-Results (PforR)
<!-- page:16 -->

<!-- p:1 page:16 -->
Investment Project Financing (IPF) is the IBRD's traditional instrument. It finances a ring-fenced set of project activities, such as civil works, equipment and consulting services. Money is disbursed as the borrower incurs eligible project expenditures [14]. IPF suits physical infrastructure with a clear scope and timetable. Both case studies in Chapter 3, TEP and CCSEP, were financed through IPF.

<!-- p:2 page:16 -->
Development Policy Financing (DPF) supports a set of policy and institutional reforms rather than a project. The borrower must complete agreed "prior actions" before the Board approves the loan. Funds then go to the general budget and are not traced to specific expenditures [14]. Vietnam's first IBRD loan was a DPF operation: a US$500 million Public Investment Reform loan approved in December 2009 and signed on 30 December 2009 [2].

<!-- p:3 page:17 -->
Program-for-Results (PforR) was introduced in 2012. It was only the third financing instrument approved by the Bank's Board since 1944 [15]. PforR supports a government's own program and uses the country's own systems. Disbursement depends on the verified achievement of Disbursement-Linked Indicators (DLIs), not on individual expenditures [14].

<!-- table:T2 page:17 -->
**Table 2. The IBRD’s three lending instruments**

| Instrument | What it finances | Disbursed against | Best suited to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investment Project Financing (IPF) | Ring-fenced project activities: works, goods, consulting services | Eligible expenditures actually incurred | Infrastructure and other projects with a defined scope |
| Development Policy Financing (DPF) | A program of policy and institutional actions | Completion of agreed prior actions | Sector or economy-wide reforms; budget support |
| Program-for-Results (PforR) | Part of a government’s own expenditure program | Verified achievement of Disbursement-Linked Indicators | Programs where results can be measured and verified |

*Source: Authors’ compilation from Wes (2023) and Gelb et al. (2016).*

<!-- p:4 page:17 -->
The key difference lies in what the IBRD "buys" with each loan. Under IPF, it pays for inputs; under DPF, for reforms; under PforR, for results. This choice shapes the borrower's obligations, which Section 2.1.3 examines in practice.

## [1.4.2] 1.4.2. Loan terms: interest rates, maturities and fees
<!-- page:17 -->

<!-- p:1 page:17 -->
The IBRD Flexible Loan (IFL) is the Bank's leading loan product for public-sector borrowers in middle-income countries [16]. Its cost reflects the IBRD's AAA credit rating and has three parts: an interest rate, a front-end fee and a commitment fee [16].

<!-- p:2 page:17 -->
The interest rate equals a market reference rate plus a spread. The reference rate was LIBOR until its phase-out; US dollar loans now use the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR). The spread combines a contractual lending spread and a maturity premium. The premium rises with the loan's average maturity and depends on the borrower's pricing group, set by income level [17]. The IBRD suspended its fixed-spread option in 2021, so new loans carry a variable spread [17].

<!-- p:3 page:18 -->
Maturities are long by market standards. In 2014, the maximum final maturity of a standard IFL was extended from 30 to 35 years, and the maximum average maturity from 18 to 20 years [18]. For some projects, the final maturity can now reach 50 years [19]. In October 2024, the IBRD added a Short Maturity Loan with a 7-year final maturity and a 20-basis-point discount on the spread [20].

<!-- table:T3 page:18 -->
**Table 3. Main terms of the IBRD Flexible Loan**

| Term | Current setting | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Interest rate | Reference rate (SOFR for USD) + variable spread | World Bank Treasury (2020) |
| Final maturity | Up to 35 years; up to 50 years for some projects | World Bank (2014b); IBRD (2025) |
| Average maturity | Up to 20 years | World Bank (2014b) |
| Short Maturity Loan | 7-year final maturity, 20 bps spread discount | World Bank Treasury (n.d.-b) |
| Front-end fee | 0.25% of the committed amount, charged once | World Bank Treasury (n.d.-a) |
| Commitment fee | 0.25% a year on the undisbursed balance, from 60 days after signing | World Bank Treasury (n.d.-a) |

*Note: Terms are reviewed annually and may change; figures are as at October 2026.*

<!-- p:4 page:18 -->
Within these limits, the borrower chooses the currency, the grace period and the repayment schedule. TEP, for example, had a 26-year maturity with a 10-year grace period [21]. CCSEP's IBRD loan had a 29-year maturity with a 10-year grace period, while its IDA credit carried 25 years with a 5-year grace period (see Section 3.3.2).

<!-- p:5 page:18 -->
The commitment fee has a practical consequence. Because it is charged on undisbursed balances, slow disbursement raises the effective cost of the loan. This links loan terms directly to the implementation problems discussed in Chapter 3.

## [1.4.3] 1.4.3. Safeguard and procurement requirements (Environmental and Social Framework)
<!-- page:19 -->

<!-- p:1 page:19 -->
IBRD financing comes with rules on environmental and social risks and on procurement. These rules apply mainly to IPF; DPF and PforR operations follow their own assessment procedures.

<!-- p:2 page:19 -->
The Environmental and Social Framework (ESF) was approved by the Board on 4 August 2016. It applies to all IPF projects initiated on or after 1 October 2018 [22]. Earlier projects continue under the older Safeguard Policies, such as OP 4.12 on involuntary resettlement. The two systems are expected to run in parallel for about seven years [23]. This is why TEP and CCSEP, both prepared before 2018, followed the Safeguard Policies.

<!-- p:3 page:19 -->
The ESF shifts more responsibility to the borrower. It sets out ten Environmental and Social Standards (ESSs) that borrowers must meet throughout the project [22]:

10. ESS10: Stakeholder Engagement and Information Disclosure

<!-- p:14 page:19 -->
Procurement follows a separate framework. The Procurement Framework applies to projects with a concept note on or after 1 July 2016 [24]. It replaced the earlier Procurement Guidelines and allows procurement approaches tailored to each project. Each new project prepares a Project Procurement Strategy for Development (PPSD), as CCSEP did. TEP, approved in 2014, still followed the earlier guidelines.

<!-- p:15 page:20 -->
These requirements are often stricter than domestic rules, especially on land compensation and resettlement. Where the two systems differ, projects face delays or, as in Nha Trang, the loss of financing (see Section 3.3.4).

## [ch2] CHAPTER 2. HOW THE IBRD SUPPORTS PROJECT FINANCING
<!-- page:21 -->

## [2.1] 2.1. Financial support
<!-- page:21 -->

<!-- p:1 page:21 -->
Financial support is the IBRD's core activity, but money reaches a project only through a fixed sequence of steps. This section follows that sequence: the project cycle, the way funds are paid out, and how each instrument works in practice.

## [2.1.1] 2.1.1. The project cycle: identification, preparation, appraisal, negotiation, approval, implementation and evaluation
<!-- page:21 -->

<!-- p:1 page:21 -->
Every IBRD operation passes through a six-stage project cycle [25]. The Bank and the borrower work closely at each stage, but their roles differ. The borrower owns and implements the project; the Bank advises, appraises, finances and supervises.

<!-- table:T4 page:21 -->
**Table 4. Stages of the World Bank project cycle**

| Stage | Main activities | Lead role | Key document |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Identification | Bank and government agree on a project concept consistent with the Country Partnership Framework | Borrower and Bank | Project Concept Note, Project Information Document |
| 2. Preparation | Feasibility studies, technical design, environmental and social instruments, procurement plan | Borrower, with Bank advice | Feasibility study, ESIA, resettlement plan |
| 3. Appraisal | Bank reviews technical, economic, financial, institutional and E&S aspects | Bank | Project Appraisal Document (PAD) |
| 4. Negotiation and Board approval | Legal agreements negotiated; Board of Executive Directors approves; loan signed and declared effective | Borrower and Bank | Loan Agreement |
| 5. Implementation and supervision | Borrower carries out the project; Bank supervises and may restructure | Borrower; Bank supervises | Implementation Status and Results Report (ISR) |
| 6. Completion and evaluation | Results assessed; lessons recorded; independent validation | Bank, then IEG | Implementation Completion and Results Report (ICR) |

*Source: Authors’ compilation from [25].*

<!-- p:2 page:22 -->
The cycle does not end with approval. During implementation, a project can be restructured when its scope, costs or timing change. TEP was restructured in 2019 and extended by two years. CCSEP was restructured in 2022, when about US$45.6 million of loan funds were cancelled (see Sections 3.2.5 and 3.3.2).

<!-- p:3 page:22 -->
In Vietnam, each stage also runs alongside domestic procedures: approval of the project proposal, the investment decision, the allocation of counterpart funds and, for local projects, on-lending agreements (see Section 3.1.4). Delays often arise at the joint between the two systems. CCSEP, for example, became effective about five and a half months later than planned (see Section 3.3.3).

## [2.1.2] 2.1.2. Disbursement mechanisms and counterpart funding requirements
<!-- page:22 -->

<!-- p:1 page:22 -->
Once a loan is effective, the IBRD releases funds through four disbursement methods, set out in each project's disbursement letter [26; 27].

<!-- table:T5 page:22 -->
**Table 5. IBRD disbursement methods for investment projects**

| Method | How it works | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Advance | Bank deposits funds in a borrower’s designated account; the borrower pays eligible expenses and documents them later | Many small and recurring payments |
| Reimbursement | Borrower pays first from its own funds; the Bank then refunds eligible expenditures | Expenses the borrower can pre-finance |
| Direct payment | Bank pays a supplier, contractor or consultant directly at the borrower’s request | Large contract payments |
| Special commitment | Bank commits to pay a third party under a letter of credit opened by a commercial bank | Imported goods and equipment |

*Source: Authors’ compilation from [26;27].*

<!-- p:2 page:23 -->
Each withdrawal must be supported by documents such as statements of expenditure or interim financial reports. The Bank also sets a minimum value for applications and a disbursement deadline, usually four months after the closing date [26]. CCSEP used all four methods, with a designated account for each province (see Section 3.3.2).

<!-- p:3 page:23 -->
The IBRD rarely finances the whole project. The borrower contributes counterpart funding, which typically covers costs the Bank does not finance. In CCSEP, counterpart funds paid for land compensation, resettlement, project staff salaries, operating costs and training (see Section 3.3.2). In TEP, NPT provided US$231.25 million of the planned US$731.25 million [21].

<!-- p:4 page:23 -->
Counterpart funding is often the binding constraint on disbursement. In CCSEP, provincial budget allocations began only in July 2019, so there was no disbursement in 2017 and 2018 (see Section 3.3.3). In such cases, IBRD money is available but cannot be used. Where loans are on-lent to provinces, local governments also take on real debt obligations, which adds to their caution.

## [2.1.3] 2.1.3. How each instrument works in practice: IPF (structure and disbursement), DPF (promoting policy reforms), PforR (linking funding to results)
<!-- page:23 -->

<!-- p:1 page:23 -->
Investment Project Financing is organized around components, a results framework and an implementing agency. Each component has its own budget and financing source, and disbursement follows the progress of contracts. TEP illustrates the model: three components, implemented by NPT's regional project management boards, with IBRD funds paid against works and equipment (see Section 3.2.2). Because disbursement tracks physical progress, delays in land clearance or bidding slow the flow of funds directly.

<!-- p:2 page:24 -->
Development Policy Financing works through a policy matrix. The government completes prior actions, such as issuing a decree or adopting a new procedure, and the loan is then released to the budget. Vietnam's Public Investment Reform (PIR) series shows how this works. The first operation, a US$500 million IBRD loan, was approved in December 2009 as the first of two single-tranche operations [2]. The second, approved in May 2011, combined an IBRD loan of US$87.3 million with an IDA credit of US$262.7 million [28]. Its reforms aimed to strengthen the public investment cycle and the legal basis for public-private partnerships [29; 30]. A similar approach supported power-sector reform: in 2010, a DPF operation combined US$200 million from the IBRD with US$111.8 million from IDA [31].

<!-- p:3 page:24 -->
Program-for-Results links money to measurable results. The borrower and the Bank agree on Disbursement-Linked Indicators, and funds are released only after an agreed agency verifies that each target is met. Because spending is not traced item by item, PforR relies on the country's own fiduciary and environmental systems, which are assessed beforehand. IEG has found that the choice of DLIs is critical: they must lead clearly to the program's development objective, and monitoring should not focus on DLIs alone [32].

<!-- p:4 page:24 -->
The three instruments therefore answer different needs. IPF builds assets, DPF supports reforms, and PforR rewards the performance of existing programs. For Vietnam, which has mainly used IPF for infrastructure, the choice matters: a results-based design shifts more responsibility, and more risk, onto domestic agencies.

## [2.2] 2.2. Technical support
<!-- page:24 -->

<!-- p:1 page:24 -->
Technical support is what distinguishes IBRD financing from a commercial loan. The Bank helps design the project before money is committed, advises on policy outside any single loan, and tracks results until well after completion.

## [2.2.1] 2.2.1. Project preparation and feasibility studies
<!-- page:24 -->

<!-- p:1 page:25 -->
During preparation, the borrower designs the project with advice from a Bank task team that includes technical, financial, procurement and safeguard specialists. The team helps define the components and check the technical design. It also tests the project's economic and financial viability. For TEP, the appraisal estimated an economic internal rate of return (EIRR) of 31.4% (see Section 3.2.4). Such analysis gives the borrower an independent check before it takes on debt.

<!-- p:2 page:25 -->
Preparation also produces the environmental and social instruments required by the Bank's policies. These include impact assessments, resettlement plans and stakeholder consultations. CCSEP, for instance, prepared an environmental and social impact assessment for each city and a resettlement policy framework for all four [33]. Where preparation itself is costly, the borrower can use a Project Preparation Advance, which is later refinanced from the loan [27].

<!-- p:3 page:25 -->
The quality of preparation shapes everything that follows. IEG concluded that more thorough advance preparation and regulatory coordination on TEP's smart-grid component would have avoided the need to extend the project (see Section 3.2.3).

## [2.2.2] 2.2.2. Analytical and Advisory Services (ASA) for policy formulation
<!-- page:25 -->

<!-- p:1 page:25 -->
Advisory Services and Analytics (ASA) are the Bank's non-lending activities that help clients advance a development objective. Their outputs include analytical reports, policy notes, hands-on advice and knowledge-sharing workshops [34]. ASA can inform a country's policies directly, or prepare the ground for later loans.

<!-- p:2 page:25 -->
ASA is funded in three ways. In 2023, analytical and advisory disbursements totalled US$991 million: 26% from the Bank's administrative budget, 68% from donor trust funds and 6% from Reimbursable Advisory Services [35]. Under Reimbursable Advisory Services (RAS), a client requests and pays for the advice under a legal agreement [34]. RAS is offered mainly to middle- and high-income countries, which makes it increasingly relevant to Vietnam [36].

<!-- p:3 page:25 -->
In Vietnam, analytical work has supported several of the reforms later financed by loans. The Public Investment Reform series, for example, rested on policy dialogue about the public investment cycle [2]. At project level, CCSEP drew on technical assistance from PPIAF (US$350,000) and GIZ to support institutional reform under Decree No. 80/2014/ND-CP (see Section 3.3.3).

## [2.2.3] 2.2.3. Monitoring and evaluation (M&E) frameworks
<!-- page:26 -->

<!-- p:1 page:26 -->
Every IBRD project is monitored against a results framework set at appraisal. The framework defines the project development objective (PDO), its outcome indicators, intermediate indicators, baselines and targets. CCSEP, for example, set three key outcome indicators: about 290,000 direct beneficiaries, 256,000 people with sewer connections, and 100% cost recovery (see Section 3.3.1).

<!-- p:2 page:26 -->
M&E then works at three levels:

<!-- p:3 page:26 -->
1. Supervision: the Bank prepares regular Implementation Status and Results Reports (ISRs), rating progress towards the PDO and implementation performance (World Bank, n.d.-c).

<!-- p:4 page:26 -->
2. Completion: at closing, the Bank prepares an Implementation Completion and Results Report (ICR) comparing results with targets.

<!-- p:5 page:26 -->
3. Independent evaluation: the Independent Evaluation Group (IEG) validates the ICR and, for selected projects, prepares a more detailed Project Performance Assessment Report (PPAR).

<!-- p:6 page:26 -->
This system produces evidence that the borrower can use. TEP's PPAR rated the outcome highly satisfactory, Bank performance satisfactory and the quality of M&E substantial [37]. Ratings during implementation also act as an early warning. In 2022, CCSEP's likelihood of achieving its PDO was downgraded to moderately unsatisfactory, which led to restructuring (see Section 3.3.3).

<!-- p:7 page:26 -->
M&E has limits. Many indicators, such as transmission losses, measure the whole system rather than the project alone. IEG therefore warned that some of TEP's improvements cannot be attributed to the project alone (see Section 3.2.4). For projects without a published ICR, such as CCSEP, the assessment must rely on interim reports.

## [2.3] 2.3. Training and Capacity Building
<!-- page:26 -->

<!-- p:1 page:27 -->
Training and capacity building aim to leave the borrower able to manage similar investments without outside help. They work at three levels: the people in project units, the technical skills those people apply, and the institutions they work for.

## [2.3.1] 2.3.1. Human resource development for project management units (PMUs)
<!-- page:27 -->

<!-- p:1 page:27 -->
In Vietnam, IBRD projects are run by project management units (PMUs) or boards attached to the implementing agency. TEP was implemented through NPT's northern, central and southern project management boards. CCSEP used one PMU in each province (see Sections 3.2.1 and 3.3.1).

<!-- p:2 page:27 -->
The Bank supports these units with training in project management, financial management, procurement and safeguards. Much of this is delivered through the World Bank's Open Learning Campus, which offers self-paced and blended courses. Its project orientation courses, for example, cover Bank expectations, project and financial management, procurement, environmental and social risk management, and M&E [38].

<!-- p:3 page:27 -->
Training is most effective when staff stay with the unit. CCSEP built on PMUs inherited from an earlier project, and the new PMU in Phan Rang drew 10 of its 12 staff from other ODA units (see Section 3.3.3). Even so, weak PMU capacity remained a main cause of delay, and the Bank recommended adding qualified staff after restructuring.

## [2.3.2] 2.3.2. Knowledge transfer in procurement and environmental safeguards
<!-- page:27 -->

<!-- p:1 page:27 -->
Much of the knowledge transfer happens through doing. Borrower staff apply the Bank's procurement rules and safeguard policies on real contracts, while Bank specialists review key steps. Over time, this builds skills in bidding, contract management and risk assessment.

<!-- p:2 page:27 -->
The Bank also runs dedicated programs. In 2014, it launched what it described as the world's first massive open online course (MOOC) on public procurement, with more than 540 certified learners [39]. E-procurement courses on the Open Learning Campus offer certification to participants who pass an examination [40].

<!-- p:3 page:28 -->
TEP shows the cumulative effect. IEG found that NPT's regional boards had built capabilities in procurement, contract administration, financial management, safeguards and reporting, partly from earlier World Bank projects [37]. Safeguards proved harder to transfer. Nha Trang shows that knowing the Bank's resettlement standards is not enough when local compensation rules and land prices differ (see Section 3.3.4).

## [2.3.3] 2.3.3. Strengthening institutional governance in beneficiary sectors
<!-- page:28 -->

<!-- p:1 page:28 -->
The third level targets institutions rather than individuals. Here, IBRD projects include components that change how a sector agency or utility is run.

<!-- table:T6 page:28 -->
**Table 6. Institutional reform activities in the two case studies**

| Project | Reform activity | Position at the latest report |
|---|---|---|
| TEP | Asset management system for NPT | Fully operational; used for investment and maintenance decisions |
| TEP | Cost-reflective transmission tariff, IFRS reporting, testing laboratory | Carried out mainly with NPT/EVN funds |
| CCSEP | Public Service Management Units in each city | Established |
| CCSEP | Wastewater tariff roadmaps, household connection policies | Approved or issued; implementation varies by city |
| CCSEP | Performance-based service contracts | Largely not yet in use, except at the Nha Trang treatment plant |

*Source: Sections 3.2.3 and 3.3.3, based on World Bank (2022a, 2022b, 2022c, 2023).*

<!-- p:2 page:28 -->
Development Policy Financing pursues the same goal at national level. The Public Investment Reform series, for example, supported measures to strengthen how public investment projects are selected and implemented [2].

<!-- p:3 page:28 -->
Institutional reform depends heavily on the borrower's own commitment. In TEP, US$5.50 million of the capacity-building component's US$5.74 million actual cost came from NPT, and only US$0.24 million from the IBRD (see Section 3.2.2). In CCSEP, the whole of Component 4 was financed by counterpart funds, and many reforms stopped at the setup stage (see Section 3.3.3). The IBRD can design and advise on reform, but the borrower must fund and sustain it.

## [2.4] 2.4. Overall assessment
<!-- page:29 -->

<!-- p:1 page:29 -->
The three forms of support are designed as one package: finance pays for the investment, technical support makes it sound, and training makes it last. Each covers a weakness of the others, and each appears at every stage of the project cycle.

<!-- table:T7 page:29 -->
**Table 7. The three forms of IBRD support across the project cycle**

| Stage | Financial support | Technical support | Training and capacity building |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preparation and appraisal | Loan size, instrument and terms agreed; Project Preparation Advance if needed | Feasibility, economic analysis, safeguard instruments, results framework | Assessment of the implementing agency’s capacity |
| Implementation | Disbursement against eligible expenditures or results | Supervision, ISRs, restructuring when needed | PMU training in procurement, financial management and safeguards |
| Completion and after | Loan repayment; savings cancelled | ICR and IEG evaluation; lessons for new projects | Systems and institutions left in place (e.g. TEP’s asset management system) |

*Source: Authors’ synthesis of Sections 2.1-2.3.*

<!-- p:2 page:29 -->
The forms reinforce one another in three ways. First, money gives weight to advice: a borrower is more likely to adopt the Bank’s procurement and safeguard standards when they are conditions of a loan. Second, advice protects the money: appraisal and supervision reduce the risk that funds are wasted on a poorly designed project. Third, training protects the results: assets built with IBRD funds keep working only if local institutions can operate and maintain them.

<!-- p:3 page:30 -->
The package, however, depends on the borrower. The evidence in Chapter 3 shows that the binding constraints usually lie on the domestic side: counterpart funding, land acquisition and PMU capacity. When these fail, IBRD money stays undisbursed, technical advice cannot be applied, and training stops at setup. The value of the IBRD therefore lies less in any single form of support than in their combination, and that combination works only when domestic systems are ready to absorb it. Chapter 3 tests this argument against two projects in Vietnam.

## [ch3] CHAPTER 3. THE IBRD AND SELECTED PROJECTS IN VIETNAM
<!-- page:31 -->

## [3.1] 3.1. Context of Vietnam-World Bank Relations
<!-- page:31 -->

<!-- p:1 page:31 -->
In the following analysis, monetary amounts are expressed in USD. Appraisal figures refer to planned financing, whereas amounts at project closing describe reported expenditure or disbursement. Distinguishing these measures is essential when assessing the IBRD contribution to project financing [44; 41; 37].

## [3.1.1] 3.1.1. Resumption of the partnership in 1993
<!-- page:31 -->

<!-- p:1 page:31 -->
The present lending relationship between Vietnam and the World Bank took shape when the Bank resumed lending in October 1993, following the resolution of arrears and the re-establishment of external financial relations. Two IDA credits were approved that month: approximately USD 70 million for primary education and USD 158.5 million for highway rehabilitation. The Bank opened its resident office in Hanoi in 1994. This re-engagement coincided with the early institutional and market reforms introduced under Đổi Mới and signalled the return of large-scale multilateral financing for infrastructure and human development [45; 46].

<!-- p:2 page:31 -->
During the following two decades, the World Bank's role extended beyond the provision of capital. IDA-financed operations supported physical infrastructure, education, poverty reduction and public-sector institutions, while policy analysis and project-level technical assistance helped public agencies manage complex investments. The historical distinction matters for this paper: "World Bank financing" is an umbrella description; the institutional source of a particular loan may be IDA or IBRD and must be identified from the project's appraisal and legal documentation rather than inferred from the World Bank name.

## [3.1.2] 3.1.2. IDA graduation in July 2017 and the transition to IBRD lending
<!-- page:31 -->

<!-- p:1 page:31 -->
Vietnam graduated from regular IDA eligibility with effect from 1 July 2017. In operational terms, graduation marked a change in the expected composition and terms of new sovereign borrowing rather than an immediate termination of all IDA-related activity. The World Bank's 2019 Performance and Learning Review records transitional IDA18 support of SDR 1.593 billion (approximately USD 2.3 billion before a subsequent allocation reduction), alongside continuing access to IBRD financing [3]. Existing IDA-financed projects therefore continued into subsequent years.

<!-- p:2 page:32 -->
The transition is relevant to project financing because IBRD lending is generally closer to market-based pricing than traditional IDA credits, making debt-service capacity, project selection, economic returns and fiscal risk more prominent considerations. Yet the transition did not establish IBRD as an entirely new lender to Vietnam. The Transmission Efficiency Project examined in Section 3.2 was approved for an IBRD loan on 7 August 2014, almost three years before IDA graduation. It therefore demonstrates that an IBRD-financed project could coexist with an active IDA portfolio before the formal graduation date [44; 41].

<!-- p:3 page:32 -->
Graduation should consequently be described as a shift in lending mix and financing conditions rather than a simple substitution of every IDA project by an IBRD project. Differences between the two institutions are most meaningful when traced to actual financing agreements, counterpart obligations and project results, rather than broad labels such as "soft loan" or "commercial loan". The World Bank Group reclassified Vietnam as an upper-middle-income economy in July 2026, a country-income classification distinct from its 2017 IDA graduation [4].

## [3.1.3] 3.1.3. Comparison with a late IDA-financed project: SAHEP (2017)
<!-- page:32 -->

<!-- p:1 page:32 -->
The Support for Autonomous Higher Education Project (SAHEP, P156849) provides a useful near-graduation comparison. Approved by the World Bank's Board on 15 May 2017, SAHEP was financed through an IDA credit of SDR 114.3 million, equivalent to USD 155 million at appraisal. Its total estimated project cost was USD 174.60 million, including USD 19.60 million of counterpart financing. The project sought to improve the research, teaching and institutional capacity of selected autonomous universities and strengthen national higher-education management [47].

<!-- p:2 page:32 -->
SAHEP is illustrative of the late IDA pipeline, not evidence that IDA disbursements stopped in July 2017 or that SAHEP was necessarily the final IDA-approved project. Moreover, its education-sector outputs cannot be compared directly with the electrical network indicators of TEP. The comparison is institutional: in 2017 an IDA credit was approved for university and governance investments, whereas TEP had already used an IBRD loan to support a large network-investment program in 2014.

## [3.1.4] 3.1.4. Legal framework governing ODA and concessional external loans
<!-- page:33 -->

<!-- p:1 page:33 -->
Multilateral borrowing is implemented within two overlapping systems: Vietnamese public-investment and public-debt rules, and the World Bank requirements incorporated in the relevant financing agreement and project documentation. The domestic framework governs such matters as project approval, external-loan administration, on-lending and allocation of counterpart funds. The Bank framework addresses eligible expenditure, procurement, financial management, reporting, safeguards and supervision. A project can encounter implementation difficulties where the timing and documentation requirements of these systems do not align.

<!-- p:2 page:34 -->
The legislation must be read as of the relevant period. At the time of TEP approval in 2014, the overarching ODA and concessional-loan decree was Decree No. 38/2013/ND-CP [48]. It was subsequently replaced by Decree No. 16/2016/ND-CP [49], and later by Decree No. 56/2020/ND-CP [50]. Decree No. 114/2021/ND-CP, as amended by Decree No. 20/2023/ND-CP, is useful for understanding an earlier phase of ODA administration but should not be presented as the current principal decree [51; 52].

<!-- p:3 page:34 -->
As at October 2026, Decree No. 242/2025/ND-CP (effective 10 September 2025), as amended by Decree No. 119/2026/ND-CP (issued 3 April 2026), forms the principal current decree-level framework [53; 54]. The 2026 amendment contains transitional provisions, making the date at which a project proposal or investment decision was approved material to procedural treatment. Since TEP closed on 31 December 2021, this contemporary regime is contextual rather than a basis for retrospectively judging the project's 2014 legal compliance.

The World Bank's environmental and social regime also changed over time. Its Environmental and Social Framework (ESF) applies to new investment project financing initiated on or after 1 October 2018 [22]. TEP, initiated before that date and classified as a Category B project, was governed by the earlier safeguard policies and related project instruments. Applying the later ESF standards retroactively to the 2014 operation would be methodologically incorrect [44].

<!-- p:5 page:35 -->
This historical and regulatory context provides the basis for assessing the IBRD in practice. The first case concerns an energy-transmission investment approved before IDA graduation, allowing the analysis to distinguish the financing mechanism from the timing of Vietnam's transition to IBRD borrowing.

## [3.2] 3.2. Case study 1: Transmission Efficiency Project (TEP), energy sector
<!-- page:35 -->

## [3.2.1] 3.2.1. Project overview
<!-- page:35 -->

<!-- p:1 page:35 -->
The Transmission Efficiency Project (TEP; P131558; IBRD loan 8417-VN) was approved on 7 August 2014 and became effective on 10 February 2015. The original closing date of 31 December 2019 was extended to 31 December 2021. Its formal development objective was to improve the capacity, efficiency and reliability of electricity transmission services in selected parts of Vietnam's network. TEP was financed using Investment Project Financing (IPF), rather than a development-policy or results-based lending instrument [44; 41; 37].

<!-- p:2 page:35 -->
The Socialist Republic of Vietnam was the borrower. The National Power Transmission Corporation (NPT), a subsidiary of Vietnam Electricity (EVN), served as the responsible implementing entity, with implementation distributed across the northern, central and southern regional project management boards. The Ministry of Industry and Trade was responsible for sector policy and relevant technical approvals. The investment program covered 220-kV and 500-kV lines and substations in the greater Hanoi region, the greater Ho Chi Minh City and Mekong Delta areas, and central Vietnam [44; 37].

<!-- p:3 page:36 -->
At appraisal, the overall project cost was estimated at USD 731.25 million, including a USD 500.00 million IBRD loan and USD 231.25 million in borrower resources. These amounts describe the planned financing envelope, not the amount drawn from IBRD or spent on physical works. The loan also included a USD 1.25 million front-end fee, so the figure for investment components alone must not be equated with the complete financing total [44; 41].

<!-- p:1 page:39 -->
The third component was intended to strengthen NPT's transition towards a more capable and commercially sustainable transmission utility. At design stage, it comprised four strands: an asset management system (AMS), assistance on cost-reflective transmission tariffs, improvement in financial reporting under International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), and improvements to the electrical testing laboratory. The project's financing and implementation experience, however, was considerably narrower than this original list suggests [44; 41; 37].

The post-completion trend limits a simplistic success narrative. Transmission losses fell to 2.15% in 2019 but subsequently rose to 2.29% in 2021 and 2.54% in 2022. <!-- p:5 page:44 -->
Renewable-energy integration should be treated with particular care. TEP's greater transfer capacity and digital substation controls improved the technical conditions for accommodating intermittent generation. IEG cites, among other examples, the Quang Ngai–Quy Nhon 220-kV line's contribution to better use of electricity from the Thuong Kon Tum hydropower plant. However, the evaluation does not quantify the additional megawatt-hours of solar or wind power integrated solely as a result of TEP. The same assessment documents continued curtailment and congestion as the supply mix changed. Accordingly, the defensible conclusion is that TEP supported the network's capability to integrate renewables, not that it eliminated renewable curtailment or achieved a verified numerical increase in renewable generation [37, pp. 8–13].

<!-- p:6 page:44 -->
The World Bank's 2022 completion report re-estimated an aggregate economic internal rate of return (EIRR) of 52.6%, compared with 31.4% in its appraisal economic analysis, with an economic net present value of around USD 1,089 million at the assumed 10% discount rate. These estimates are results of a cost-benefit model and not an observed cash return to IBRD or a guaranteed financial rate of return for NPT. The ICR associated the higher re-estimated EIRR partly with substantially lower capital cost; the results are sensitive to the analytical assumptions, including the monetary value assigned to avoid supply interruptions [41, Annex 4].

## [3.2.5] 3.2.5. Implementation challenges and financing limitations
<!-- page:44 -->

<!-- p:1 page:44 -->
The distinction between the approved loan and actual IBRD-financed expenditure is central to the financing evaluation. Against the USD 500.00 million original commitment, the closing accounts report IBRD disbursement of USD 318.53 million, or 63.7% of the original amount. The arithmetic difference is USD 181.47 million (36.3%). Meanwhile, actual total project cost was USD 517.53 million, USD 213.72 million below appraisal. The original counterpart allocation of USD 231.25 million and the final amount of approximately USD 199.00 million are also different [41, Annex 3; 37, p. v].

<!-- p:2 page:45 -->
The size of the unused envelope should not automatically be labeled an implementation failure. Lower steel prices, competition in procurement and reductions in planned activities all contributed to the smaller financing requirement. IEG specifically refers to approximately USD 160 million of loan savings cancelled at closure; this is a reported cancellation figure, not a substitute for the USD 181.47 million calculated difference between the original commitment and actual disbursement. Those two numbers describe different accounting concepts and should not be treated as interchangeable without a full reconciliation of loan-account transactions [37, pp. x, 17].

The schedule overrun was more directly connected to implementation shortcomings. The selection and regulatory approval of smart-grid technology, including concerns about taking substations offline during upgrades, required roughly four years; Component 2 had made little progress by the 2019 restructuring. Some line and substation works were also delayed by bidding-related processing and compensation and land acquisition. The project therefore closed two years later than originally planned. The independent assessment nevertheless rated the overall outcome highly satisfactory because the principal capacity, efficiency and reliability objectives were substantially achieved [42, pp. 3, 12; 37, pp. x–xi, 17–18].

<!-- p:4 page:46 -->
Environmental and social issues remained material. The independent assessment records approximately 2,500 affected households, including 80 relocated households, and notes unresolved compensation or resettlement cases subject to post-closure follow-up. This evidence supports a differentiated assessment: the project's engineering and economic results were strong, while safeguards, land access and inter-agency coordination caused important administrative costs. Progress on physical outputs alone did not resolve these institutional constraints [37, pp. x–xi, Appendix B].

<!-- p:5 page:46 -->
Finally, sustainability depends on factors outside the project boundary. IEG warned that NPT's ability to maintain and expand the network would depend on more adequate transmission tariffs and timely subsequent capital investment, particularly as variable renewable generation expanded. Thus the TEP experience illustrates the complementary role of finance, technical assistance and managerial capacity, but also the limits of project-specific investment when national network planning and tariffs lag demand and generation growth [37, pp. 18–20].

## [3.2.6] 3.2.6. Overall assessment of the TEP case
<!-- page:46 -->

<!-- p:1 page:46 -->
TEP shows how a sovereign IBRD investment loan can combine high-voltage capital expenditure with technology modernization and targeted institutional development. Its strongest demonstrable achievements were in physical outputs and improved operating indicators. It also delivered a useful reduction in measured capital costs relative to the appraisal budget. These strengths should be weighed against the lengthy smart-grid preparation process, the large difference between loan commitment and final disbursement, the concentration of capacity-building costs on the counterpart side, and continuing sector-wide network constraints.

<!-- p:2 page:47 -->
For the broader research question, three conclusions follow. First, IBRD participation existed alongside IDA before the 2017 graduation date and cannot be treated as a purely post-IDA arrangement. Second, the financing mechanism should be evaluated using both approved and disbursed funds, with borrower expenditure shown separately. Third, the project's successful outputs do not establish that all subsequent improvements in Vietnam's transmission system, or the integration of a specific quantity of renewable generation, were caused by TEP. These distinctions make the case study an evidence-based foundation for the comparison with the urban and environmental project in Section 3.3.

<!-- p:3 page:47 -->
In the next case study, the Coastal Cities Sustainable Environment Project is examined as a contrasting urban and environmental operation. Whereas TEP relied on a large IBRD loan and was implemented through NPT, CCSEP combined IBRD and IDA financing with decentralized provincial implementation. The comparison therefore highlights how funding structure, counterpart obligations and institutional capacity influence implementation across sectors.

## [3.3] 3.3. Case study 2: Coastal Cities Sustainable Environment Project (CCSEP), urban and environment sector
<!-- page:47 -->

## [3.3.1] 3.3.1. Project overview
<!-- page:47 -->

<!-- p:1 page:47 -->
In the 2010s, rapid urbanization, combined with limited financial resources in a lower-middle-income economy, placed severe strain on technical infrastructure systems and environmental sanitation services in Vietnam. At that time, only about 10% of urban wastewater was treated, and combined drainage systems frequently overflowed during the rainy season, discharging untreated wastewater onto the streets and beaches of coastal cities. The sector's investment requirement was estimated at around USD 771 million per year, while expected funding covered only about 27% [33]. Facing this gap, the project required long-term capital from the World Bank, combining an IBRD loan with IDA concessional credits to provide provinces with sufficient upfront investment capital.

<!-- p:2 page:48 -->
Against this backdrop, the Vietnam Coastal Cities Sustainable Environment Project (CCSEP, P156143) was appraised by the World Bank in April 2017 under the Investment Project Financing mechanism. It continued the earlier Coastal Cities Environmental Sanitation Project (CCESP, 2007–2014) in Dong Hoi, Quy Nhon and Nha Trang, while expanding to Phan Rang–Thap Cham. The project was approved by the World Bank on 5 May 2017; the financing agreement was signed on 27 October 2017 and became effective on 18 January 2018. According to the appraisal schedule, the project was planned to run from 5 May 2017 to 30 June 2022, with loan closing on 31 December 2022. However, this deadline was later extended, and the loan officially closed on 30 June 2024. The total project cost at appraisal was USD 273.6 million. The borrower was the Government of Vietnam, the World Bank served as the financier, and implementation was decentralized to the four provinces of Quang Binh, Binh Dinh, Khanh Hoa and Ninh Thuan through Project Management Units (PMUs) under the direction of provincial People's Committees [33; 43].

## [3.3.2] 3.3.2. Project components and financial structure
<!-- page:49 -->

<!-- p:1 page:49 -->
CCSEP was designed as an Investment Project Financing operation, financing specific investment items rather than policy reforms or results, as the other two instruments do [33, Data Sheet]. Total project cost at appraisal was USD 273.6 million, of which World Bank financing was USD 236.2 million (86.3%) and counterpart funding from Vietnam was USD 37.4 million (13.7%) [33, Data Sheet, para. 43]. The World Bank portion was a blended financing package: an IBRD loan of USD 46 million (16.8%) alongside an IDA credit of USD 190.2 million (69.5%) [33, para. 66]. This combination reflected Vietnam's status at the time as a lower-middle-income country on blend terms, shortly before its IDA graduation [33, paras. 1, 44].

<!-- figure:F5 page:49 -->
**Figure 5. Project financing structure as planned at appraisal** *(chart image in the PDF; data rebuilt in content/charts.yaml → F5)*

*Source: projects.worldbank.org*

<!-- p:2 page:49 -->
The terms of the two financing sources differed significantly. The IBRD loan was denominated in USD, with a floating interest rate then based on LIBOR, a 29-year maturity including a 10-year grace period, and equal principal repayments. The IDA credit was denominated in SDR, with a 25-year maturity including a 5-year grace period, an interest rate of 2% per annum (0.75% service charge and 1.25% interest), a commitment fee of 0.5% per annum, and no front-end fee [33, para. 44]. Under the Government's ODA on-lending policy, the entire IBRD loan was on-lent to the four provinces, whereas the IDA credit was split into on-granting and on-lending portions depending on the economic conditions of each province: 80% on-granted to Dong Hoi and Phan Rang, 70% to Quy Nhon, and 50% to Nha Trang [33, para. 45]. As a result, approximately 49.1% of the total project cost was on-granted to local authorities, 37.2% was on-lent, and 13.7% was counterpart funding [33, Annex 5, Table 5.1]. For the provinces, the on-lent funds, namely the entire IBRD loan and about 29.4% of the IDA credit (about USD 101.8 million in total), represented genuine debt service obligations. The PAD warned that the project would significantly increase the debt burden of all four provinces, with Binh Dinh projected to slightly exceed its debt ceiling during 2018–2021 [33, paras. 66, 75–76, Annex 5].

## [3.3.3] 3.3.3. Project implementation
<!-- page:54 -->

<!-- p:1 page:54 -->
The project implementation process was slower than planned in terms of both timeline and disbursement. The financing agreement became effective on 18 January 2018, about five and a half months behind the original schedule of 3 August 2017, and the loan closing date had to be extended by 18 months, from 31 December 2022 to 30 June 2024 (Table 3.3.1.1) [33; 55; 43]. Disbursement lagged even further behind. The PAD projected a cumulative disbursement of USD 212.3 million by the end of FY2022; however, by late 2022 the IBRD had disbursed only USD 16.47 million and IDA USD 68.34 million, totalling approximately USD 84.8 million, equivalent to 36% of the original loan capital. The root cause of this delay was counterpart funding: budget allocations were made only from July 2019, resulting in zero disbursement in 2017 and 2018, while compensation, site clearance and contractor capacity issues continued to slow progress in subsequent years. Although signed contracts reached USD 160.9 million, physical construction volume accounted for only about 45% of total contract value [43]. Thus, World Bank capital was not lacking but could not be disbursed because of bottlenecks in counterpart funding and contract execution. The actual figures in this section are as of 31 October 2022, the restructuring date, as the project completion report has not yet been published.

Technical support. The project was classified as Category A for environmental and social safeguards, subject to the involuntary resettlement policy OP 4.12 and procurement under World Bank guidelines [33]. The World Bank provided support through appraisal, periodic supervision, progress ratings and restructuring decisions as needed. At the end of 2022, implementation progress was rated Moderately Satisfactory, but the likelihood of achieving the development objective was downgraded to Moderately Unsatisfactory. Procurement, financial management and safeguards were all rated Moderately Satisfactory, and legal covenants were fully complied with [43]. The project was delayed by a combination of factors: slow site clearance and compensation, particularly in Nha Trang and Phan Rang; weak consultant and contractor capacity; major storms in 2020; the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020–2021; and weak contract management reflected in delayed contract extensions, payments and completion certificates.

<!-- p:3 page:55 -->
Component 3 on resettlement experienced the most significant bottlenecks. The PAD estimated 1,486 affected households, of which 329 required relocation [33]. In reality, in Nha Trang, the Ngoc Hiep resettlement site was only in phase 1 of construction, and only 160 out of 585 compensation plans had been approved. A third of the approved plans were rejected by residents, causing construction to halt on most Component 2 contracts. Consequently, package NT-2.1 was partially cancelled and NT-2.2 was completely cancelled, reducing the number of affected households by approximately 35% and relocated households by about 43%. In Phan Rang, the Phan Dang Luu resettlement site was completed, with 1,400 out of 1,586 compensation plans approved, while Dong Hoi and Quy Nhon largely completed their resettlement activities [43].

This was accompanied by training in procurement, financial management and safeguards; internal audit teams; financial management manuals; governance and integrity action plans; and technical assistance from PPIAF (USD 350,000) and GIZ for institutional reform under Decree No. 80/2014/ND-CP. The goal was for each city to have a service management unit, and for the project to deliver 14 performance-based service contracts, 4 service tariff roadmaps and 4 connection policies [33].

<!-- p:5 page:56 -->
In practice, PMU capacity remained a key cause of progress delays. After the restructuring, the World Bank recommended adding qualified staff, improving coordination between PMUs and provincial departments, amending contract terms with contractors and consultants, increasing supervision frequency, and holding monthly progress meetings alongside biweekly site clearance meetings [43]. Institutionally, Public Service Management Units (PSMUs) were established in each city. Drainage and wastewater assets were transferred to People's Committees, road assets to transport departments, connection policies and septic tank sludge management plans were issued, and tariff roadmaps were approved, though implementation levels varied by locality. Performance-based service contracts remained largely unexecuted, with the exception of the Nha Trang wastewater treatment plant. Training and institutional reform efforts thus largely stopped at the setup stage without becoming fully operational.

<!-- p:6 page:56 -->
The three forms of support complemented one another but ran into the same bottleneck. World Bank funds were available but could not be disbursed while counterpart funding arrived late. Technical support helped identify resettlement risks early and address them through restructuring, but weak PMU capacity slowed compensation and contract management. Training bore fruit only when PMUs had enough staff and contracts were ready for implementation. By late 2022, the project had disbursed about 36% of its original loan capital, cancelled about USD 45.6 million, scaled back its scope in Nha Trang and needed an 18-month extension. The case also shows the limited weight of the IBRD within a blended package. The IBRD loan was only 16.8% of the planned cost (14.2% after adjustment) and fully financed Component 2, while the larger IDA credit financed the sanitation infrastructure [33, Annex 2, Table 2.1; 43].

## [3.3.4] 3.3.4. Results and challenges
<!-- page:57 -->

<!-- p:1 page:57 -->
The project loan closed on 30 June 2024, but to date an Implementation Completion and Results Report (ICR) has not been published. The information below therefore evaluates progress based on the latest publicly available data, mainly figures as of 31 October 2022 from the Restructuring Paper, supplemented by milestones for 2023–2025 from the Inspection Panel records.

<!-- p:2 page:57 -->
On these figures, the project delivered tangible results in drainage and sanitation but remained well below its appraisal targets. As Table 3.3.3.1 shows, direct beneficiaries reached 64% of the target and sewer access only 38%, while cost recovery stood at 98% of the 100% target [33; 43]. Works in Dong Hoi and Quy Nhon were largely completed, while those in Nha Trang and Phan Rang remained incomplete because of resettlement delays. Disbursement continued to rise after the restructuring and, according to the Inspection Panel, stood at 57.43% in August 2023, when the request for inspection was received [57, paras. 6, 55]. This rate may be calculated on a different base from the 36% reported in Section 3.3.3, so the two figures are not directly comparable.

<!-- p:3 page:57 -->
Regarding climate resilience, the PAD set a target to reduce flood risks for approximately 44,000 people and to construct 17 km of embankments, 22 km of roads and 5 bridges [33]. According to the latest published figures, only 4.38 km of roads and 3 bridges have been completed, while figures for embankments and the number of people with reduced flood risks are not yet publicly available. Flood reduction benefits are therefore currently demonstrated only through completed works, and full target achievement cannot yet be confirmed. More sustainable results lie in institutional capacity building. Each city established a PSMU; drainage and wastewater assets were handed over to local People's Committees; road assets were transferred to transport departments; household connection policies and septic tank sludge management plans were issued; and tariff roadmaps were approved [43]. The Nha Trang wastewater treatment plant was an exception, as it already operated under a performance-based service contract. The 38% coverage rate indicates that the benefits of drainage infrastructure are fully realized only when networks and household connections are completed.

<!-- p:4 page:58 -->
The single largest bottleneck was resettlement in Nha Trang, whose course is described in Section 3.3.3. Of the 585 households originally identified for compensation, only 160 had approved plans after the restructuring, and two work packages were delayed and then cancelled, so the works could not be completed within the project period [57, executive summary, paras. 10, 29]. The Management Response puts the number of households affected by project works in Nha Trang at about 464 [57, executive summary]. The land concerned comprises about 5 ha not yet acquired, about 6 ha acquired in 2018 and about 2.5 ha acquired in 2021 [57, paras. 11, 63]. World Bank Management cited "technical reasons" for the cancellations and stated that they would not affect achievement of the project development objective [57, paras. 49, 59].

<!-- p:5 page:58 -->
This bottleneck later led to a formal complaint. According to a request submitted by 31 residents of Ngoc Hiep Ward to the Inspection Panel on 27 August 2023, residents faced land use and construction restrictions since 2016, inconsistent resettlement policies, inadequate land compensation prices, and security pressure due to police presence [57, paras. 13, 17, 19, 23]. These represent allegations by the complainants and do not constitute conclusions. World Bank Management reviewed 222 compensation plans and identified 19 non-compliant cases, which it referred to the PMU for correction in June 2023. Eighteen of them involved low land prices and were being revised. The compensation plan for the 5 ha area was officially revoked in late August 2023 [57, paras. 37, 40, 41]. The World Bank's Board of Executive Directors approved the investigation on 5 February 2024. The dispute resolution process concluded on 7 August 2025 without a signed agreement, producing only a confidential memorandum signed on 2 March 2025. As the complainants withdrew from the process, the case returned to the Inspection Panel, and no final findings have been issued to date [58].

<!-- p:6 page:59 -->
Three direct causes lie behind these problems, as analyzed in Section 3.3.3: disagreements over land prices and compensation, which led one-third of the approved plans to be rejected; land-use restrictions imposed since 2016 without completed acquisition, which prolonged residents' waiting; and weak PMU capacity and coordination with provincial authorities, which delayed compensation and contract execution [43]. Resettlement was thus a precondition for the drainage infrastructure to operate, not an auxiliary component, and its delay carried over to sanitation and flood-control results, disbursement and contract schedules.

## [3.4.1] 3.4.1. Achievements: financial mobilization, higher technical and management standards
<!-- page:59 -->

<!-- p:1 page:59 -->
Financial mobilization. IBRD loans gave Vietnam long-term capital on terms that domestic markets could not easily match. TEP received USD 500 million with a 26-year maturity and a 10-year grace period [21], and CCSEP's IBRD loan carried a 29-year maturity [33]. Both projects also mobilized counterpart resources: NPT committed USD 231.25 million to TEP, and CCSEP's counterpart share rose to 20.8% after restructuring. In TEP, competitive bidding under Bank rules lowered capital costs, so the same objectives were achieved with less borrowing [37].

<!-- p:2 page:59 -->
Higher technical standards. TEP introduced smart-grid technology in five 500-kV and ten 220-kV substations and added 4,263 MW of transformer capacity. Operating costs per MWh fell from USD 0.87 to USD 0.67, and unserved energy dropped sharply [37]. CCSEP extended sewer networks and treatment capacity in four coastal cities that had treated only a small share of their wastewater.

<!-- p:3 page:59 -->
Higher management standards. Both projects applied the Bank's procurement, financial management and safeguard rules, and their implementing units built skills in these areas. NPT's asset management system now supports investment and maintenance decisions. In CCSEP, the reforms under Decree No. 80/2014/ND-CP produced service management units, connection policies and tariff roadmaps, and cost recovery reached 98% [43]. These changes in institutions are likely to last longer than any single project.

## [3.4.2] 3.4.2. Limitations: slow disbursement, procedural complexity, counterpart funding, gaps between WB safeguards and domestic regulations
<!-- page:60 -->

<!-- p:1 page:60 -->
Slow disbursement. Neither project used its loan as planned. TEP disbursed 63.7% of its IBRD commitment, although this partly reflected lower costs [41]. CCSEP disbursed nothing in 2017 and 2018 and only about 36% of its loan capital by late 2022 [43]. Because IBRD loans carry a commitment fee on undisbursed balances (Section 1.4.2), slow disbursement also raises the effective cost of borrowing.

<!-- p:2 page:60 -->
Procedural complexity. Projects must satisfy both Vietnamese and World Bank procedures. In TEP, the choice and approval of smart-grid technology took about four years [37]. In CCSEP, the loan became effective five and a half months late, and three disbursement conditions depended on approvals by the Ministry of Finance and provincial People's Committees [33].

<!-- p:3 page:60 -->
Counterpart funding. Domestic funding was often the binding constraint. CCSEP's provincial allocations began only in July 2019, which delayed the entire project [43]. In TEP, NPT financed almost all of the capacity-building component, USD 5.50 million of USD 5.74 million [41]. Where loans are on-lent, provinces also bear real debt, and the PAD warned that Binh Dinh could exceed its debt ceiling [33].

<!-- p:4 page:60 -->
Gaps between World Bank safeguards and domestic regulations. Land compensation proved the most serious problem. In Nha Trang, only 160 of 585 compensation plans had been approved by late 2022, residents rejected a third of the approved plans, two work packages were cancelled, and residents filed a complaint with the Inspection Panel [43; 57]. TEP also affected about 2,500 households, with some cases still unresolved after closing [37]. Domestic land prices and procedures did not always meet the Bank's resettlement standards.

## [3.4.3] 3.4.3. Recommendations for Vietnam
<!-- page:60 -->

<!-- p:1 page:60 -->
Vietnam's reclassification as an upper-middle-income economy in 2026 means that future World Bank borrowing will rely on IBRD terms [4]. Using this more expensive capital well requires changes on the domestic side, where the two cases show the main constraints lie.

<!-- p:2 page:61 -->
1. Prepare projects more fully before approval. Technology choices, technical approvals and land acquisition plans should be settled before the loan is signed. TEP's four-year delay on smart-grid technology could have been shortened by earlier regulatory coordination [37]. A Project Preparation Advance can finance this work and be refinanced from the loan.

<!-- p:3 page:61 -->
2. Secure counterpart funding at the start. Counterpart funds should be included in the medium-term public investment plan when the project is approved, not after effectiveness. Before on-lending to provinces, the Ministry of Finance should check each province's debt capacity, as the CCSEP PAD did for Binh Dinh.

<!-- p:4 page:61 -->
3. Align compensation with World Bank standards early. For new projects under the ESF, land valuation, consultation and grievance mechanisms should meet ESS5 from the preparation stage. Independent land price valuation and early consultation with affected households would reduce the risk seen in Nha Trang, where disputes over prices led to cancelled contracts and a formal complaint.

<!-- p:5 page:61 -->
4. Strengthen and stabilize PMUs. Experienced staff should be kept in professional PMUs that manage several projects over time, rather than in units created and dissolved with each loan. Training through the World Bank's Open Learning Campus should focus on contract management, procurement and safeguards.

<!-- p:6 page:61 -->
5. Streamline domestic procedures. Under Decree No. 242/2025/ND-CP, approval steps for project proposals, investment decisions, on-lending and counterpart allocation should be shortened and scheduled to match the World Bank project cycle [53].

<!-- p:7 page:61 -->
6. Choose instruments strategically. For reform programs with measurable results, Vietnam can consider PforR or DPF rather than relying only on IPF. Reimbursable Advisory Services can provide tailored advice where analytical support is needed outside a loan [36].

## [concl] CONCLUSION
<!-- page:62 -->

## [concl-1] 1. Summary of findings
<!-- page:62 -->

## [concl-1.1] 1.1. Synthesizing the three pillars of IBRD support: finance, technical support and training
<!-- page:62 -->

<!-- p:1 page:62 -->
The IBRD is the World Bank Group's lender to middle-income and creditworthy poorer countries. It raises funds on capital markets on the strength of its AAA rating and lends them on long maturities through three instruments: IPF, DPF and PforR. Its support for project financing rests on three pillars. Finance pays for the investment through the project cycle and its disbursement methods. Technical support improves project design, policy and monitoring. Training and capacity building strengthen the people and institutions that carry out projects. These pillars work as one package, and each depends on the others.

## [concl-1.2] 1.2. Re-evaluating project impacts from the case studies
<!-- page:62 -->

<!-- p:1 page:62 -->
The two case studies show both the value and the limits of this package in Vietnam. TEP shows what IBRD financing can achieve with a capable implementer: transmission outputs exceeded their targets, operating costs fell, and IEG rated the outcome highly satisfactory. It also shows that a successful project may still disburse far less than its commitment. CCSEP shows the opposite side. Late counterpart funding, weak PMU capacity and resettlement disputes kept results well below targets and led to cancelled funds and a formal complaint. Across both cases, the binding constraints lay mainly on the domestic side rather than in the availability of IBRD money.

## [concl-2] 2. Significance of the paper
<!-- page:62 -->

<!-- p:1 page:62 -->
The paper brings together the IBRD's organization, lending terms and modes of support in one framework and applies that framework consistently to two Vietnamese projects. It distinguishes planned from actual financing and IBRD funds from IDA and counterpart funds, which avoids overstating the IBRD's contribution. Its findings are relevant to Vietnam's new stage of borrowing after IDA graduation and its 2026 reclassification as an upper-middle-income economy.

## [concl-3] 3. Limitations of the research
<!-- page:63 -->

<!-- p:1 page:63 -->
The study relies on secondary data and could not verify results through field surveys or interviews. Many sources are prepared by the World Bank itself, although independent evaluations by IEG and the Inspection Panel were used where available. CCSEP has no published completion report, so its results are based on interim documents up to 2022–2025. Finally, two case studies cannot represent the whole IBRD portfolio in Vietnam, and system-wide indicators cannot be attributed to a single project.

## [concl-4] 4. Directions for further research
<!-- page:63 -->

<!-- p:1 page:63 -->
Future research could update the CCSEP assessment once its completion report is published. It could also study IBRD projects approved after IDA graduation, such as the Binh Duong water environment project and the southern waterway program. Comparing IBRD lending with that of other multilateral lenders in Vietnam, such as the Asian Development Bank, would show whether the same constraints apply. A quantitative study of disbursement delays across the portfolio could measure how much counterpart funding and land acquisition cost Vietnam in commitment fees and lost time.
